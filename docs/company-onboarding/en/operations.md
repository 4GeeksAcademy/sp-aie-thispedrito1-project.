# How we operate — the patient journey

## The five steps of the patient journey
Every visit follows the same five steps: booking, visit, clinical documentation, billing and follow-up. A problem in one step shows up later in another. For example, an insurance detail captured badly at booking becomes a denied claim at billing weeks later.

## Booking
Patients book by phone in the US and through clinic reception in the UK. There is no shared online booking system yet. At booking we confirm the reason for the visit, the clinic and, in the US, the patient's insurance plan.

## Visit and clinical documentation
During the visit the clinician examines the patient and records the encounter in the clinic's electronic health record. US and UK clinics use different record systems that do not talk to each other. Clinicians spend around 35 minutes a day on documentation tasks, which is time taken away from patients.

## Billing
In the US we bill commercial insurers, Medicare and Medicaid through claims. In the UK, billing combines private payment and a small NHS contract. A claim can only be as good as the data captured in the earlier steps.

## Follow-up
After the visit we follow up on results, referrals and the next appointment. Follow-up is where continuity of care is won or lost.

## No-shows are an operational problem
Our no-show rate across the network is 22%: about one patient in five does not attend. A no-show is a lost appointment slot that another patient could have used, a gap in someone's care and lost revenue. Proactive reminders are the main lever to reduce it.

## Referrals are an operational problem
A referral sends a patient to a specialist inside or outside HealthCore. When a referral is not tracked, the patient may never reach the specialist and we lose sight of their care. Every referral needs an owner until the patient is seen.

## Claim denials are an operational problem
Our claim denial rate is 14%, more than double the industry average. Most denials come from errors earlier in the journey: wrong eligibility data, missing documentation or coding mistakes. Fixing data quality before a claim is sent is cheaper than reworking a denied claim.

## A generic example
Patient A books a same-day appointment by phone. At booking the insurance plan is recorded with a typo. The visit goes well, but the claim is denied two weeks later because the plan does not match. Patient A also misses the follow-up because no reminder was sent. One small error at booking caused two operational problems. This example is fictional and contains no real patient data.

## In practice for HealthCore Digital {for: digital}
Every step of the journey is a data handover between systems that were never integrated. Your work reduces manual re-entry: shared data models, reminders that cut no-shows and validations that stop bad data before billing.

## In practice for Revenue Cycle {for: revenue_cycle}
You see the consequences of the whole journey in the claims. When you analyse a denial, trace it back to the step where the data went wrong, not only to the claim itself.

## In practice for other corporate teams {for: compliance, patient_experience, people, leadership}
Even if you never touch a claim or a booking, your decisions affect these steps. Ask which step of the journey a decision changes before you make it.
